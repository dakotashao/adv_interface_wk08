The Lemon Tree

from Small Weather

“It turned out the whole street had been holding a conversation in fruit.”
46

Every house on Hill Street had something it was known for. The Ortegas had the blue door, the Kims had the dog that sang along to ambulances, and we had the lemon tree, which was older than the house and, my grandmother liked to say, considerably better behaved.

It grew against the south wall, where the stucco stayed warm into the evening. In winter it was the only green thing for blocks, and in spring it gave more fruit than any family could use, so the lemons left the yard the way news does: in paper bags, in coat pockets, in the hands of children sent next door with strict instructions not to drop them.

My grandmother kept a ledger of who had received what. It was not about fairness. She simply believed that a gift should be remembered by the person who gave it, so that it could be given again, a little better, the following year.

I found the ledger after she died, in the drawer with the rubber bands and the batteries that no longer worked. Thirty-one years of lemons, written in pencil, each name followed by a number and, sometimes, a word. Mrs. Ortega, 12, for the cake. The Kim boy, 3, sore throat. Me, 40, college.

I had no idea she had counted. I had no idea anyone was keeping track of the small things, the bags left on porches, the jars of marmalade that appeared without a note. It turned out the whole street had been holding a conversation in fruit, and she had been the only one taking minutes.

That spring the tree flowered as if nothing had happened. I picked the first lemons in March and stood in the kitchen for a long time with the ledger open, trying to decide who should have them. In the end I did what she would have done. I wrote the date at the top of a new page, and I started with the people who had been there longest.

The pencil was still in the drawer. It was short and badly sharpened, and it wrote, to my surprise, exactly like her.
